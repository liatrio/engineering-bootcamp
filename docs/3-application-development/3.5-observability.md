# Observability

Tests tell you whether your code works before you ship it. Observability tells you what it is actually doing once it's running: which requests are slow, which ones fail, and why. No test suite can predict every input, load pattern, or dependency failure that production will throw at your code. When something goes wrong at 2 a.m., the telemetry your application emits is how you find out what happened.

The OpenTelemetry [observability primer](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/concepts/observability-primer/) puts it this way: "Observability lets you understand a system from the outside by letting you ask questions about that system without knowing its inner workings." To make that possible, the application has to emit **telemetry**, which is data about what it is doing. Adding the code that emits telemetry is called **instrumentation**.

At the end of 3.4 your team's `assignments-service` is tested and checked on every pull request, but it's invisible once it runs: it sends no telemetry at all. This section fixes that, and ends at milestone **M5**.

## Monitoring and Observability

**Monitoring** watches for problems you already know about: CPU above 90%, error rate above 1%, disk almost full. It answers questions you thought to ask in advance, usually with dashboards and alerts.

**Observability** goes further. A system is observable when you can answer *new* questions about its behavior, ones nobody anticipated, without shipping new code to answer them. Why are requests from one customer slow, but only on Tuesdays? Which code path produced this error? Observability needs richer telemetry than monitoring does, and it needs that telemetry to be connected, so you can move from a spike on a dashboard to the exact request that caused it.

That matters most in a system like the Company app, where one click in the browser can touch four services and a message broker. No single service's logs can tell you the whole story.

## The Three Signals

Telemetry usually comes as three kinds of signal. Each subsection covers one in depth.

| Signal | What it is | Good for | Section |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Logs | Timestamped records of discrete events | The details of what happened in one specific case | [Logging](3-application-development/3.5.1-logging.md) |
| Metrics | Numeric measurements aggregated over time | Trends, dashboards, and alerts: how much, how often, how fast | [Metrics](3-application-development/3.5.2-metrics.md) |
| Traces | The path of one request through the system, as a tree of timed operations | Where time is spent and where failures start, especially across services | [Tracing](3-application-development/3.5.3-tracing.md) |

The signals are most useful together. A metric shows that the error rate jumped. A trace from that period shows which operation failed. The logs attached to that trace explain why. [OpenTelemetry](3-application-development/3.5.4-opentelemetry.md) is the open standard most teams now use to produce all three in a consistent, connected way, and every Company app service uses it.

## What to Watch

Measuring everything is easy. Knowing what matters is harder. Three well-known starting points:

- **The four golden signals**, from the [Google SRE book](https://sre.google/sre-book/monitoring-distributed-systems/): latency, traffic, errors, and saturation. If you can measure only four things about a user-facing system, measure these.
- **The RED method**, created by Tom Wilkie for request-driven services: **R**ate (requests per second), **E**rrors (how many of those requests fail), and **D**uration (how long they take). See [The RED Method](https://grafana.com/blog/2018/08/02/the-red-method-how-to-instrument-your-services/).
- **The USE method**, from Brendan Gregg, for resources such as CPUs, disks, and queues: "For every resource, check utilization, saturation, and errors." See [The USE Method](https://www.brendangregg.com/usemethod.html).

RED describes your service from the outside, as its users experience it. USE describes the resources underneath it, such as the RabbitMQ queues the Company app's consumers read from.

## Service Levels

Telemetry becomes actionable when you decide what "good" looks like:

- A **service level indicator (SLI)** is a measurement of user experience, such as the proportion of requests that succeed, or that complete in under 300 ms.
- A **service level objective (SLO)** is a target for an SLI over a period of time, such as 99.9% of requests succeeding over 30 days.
- The **error budget** is the gap between the SLO and perfection, here 0.1% of requests. While budget remains, the team can ship changes quickly. When it runs out, reliability work comes first.

The SRE book's [Service Level Objectives](https://sre.google/sre-book/service-level-objectives/) chapter and the SRE workbook's [Implementing SLOs](https://sre.google/workbook/implementing-slos/) explain how to choose them.

The SRE book also says monitoring should answer two questions: "what's broken, and why?" The "what" is a symptom users feel, such as slow pages or failed requests. The "why" is a cause, such as a full disk or a slow database. Alert on symptoms, so every page means users are affected, and use the rest of your telemetry to find the cause.

## The Stack You Already Have

You don't install anything for this section. The Company app's [`compose.yaml`](https://github.com/liatrio/engineering-bootcamp/tree/master/examples/company-app/starter/compose.yaml) already runs [`grafana/otel-lgtm`](https://github.com/grafana/docker-otel-lgtm), one container that bundles an OpenTelemetry Collector with Loki (logs), Grafana, Tempo (traces), and Prometheus (metrics): the L, G, T, and M in its name. It is "intended for development, demo, and testing environments", which is exactly what you need here. Grafana is on <http://localhost:3001>, because the docs site uses 3000.

Every starter service sends all three signals to it over OTLP, each in the way that's idiomatic for its language:

| Service | How it's instrumented |
| --- | --- |
| employees-service (.NET) | The OpenTelemetry SDK, configured in code in [`TelemetryExtensions.cs`](https://github.com/liatrio/engineering-bootcamp/tree/master/examples/company-app/starter/employees-service/src/EmployeesService/Infrastructure/Telemetry/TelemetryExtensions.cs) |
| projects-service (Python) | Zero-code: the [`Dockerfile`](https://github.com/liatrio/engineering-bootcamp/tree/master/examples/company-app/starter/projects-service/Dockerfile) starts the app under `opentelemetry-instrument` |
| notifications-service (Go) | The Go SDK, in [`telemetry.go`](https://github.com/liatrio/engineering-bootcamp/tree/master/examples/company-app/starter/notifications-service/internal/telemetry/telemetry.go), plus hand-written trace extraction from RabbitMQ messages |
| web (React) | Browser tracing in [`telemetry.ts`](https://github.com/liatrio/engineering-bootcamp/tree/master/examples/company-app/starter/web/src/lib/telemetry.ts), sent to `/otlp/` through the frontend-proxy |

The shared settings sit at the top of `compose.yaml`, in the `x-otel` block that every service merges in: where to send telemetry (`OTEL_EXPORTER_OTLP_ENDPOINT`), how (`OTEL_EXPORTER_OTLP_PROTOCOL`), and how often to export metrics (`OTEL_METRIC_EXPORT_INTERVAL`, 5 seconds instead of the default 60, so you see results quickly).

Telemetry needs traffic. [`infra/k6/load.js`](https://github.com/liatrio/engineering-bootcamp/tree/master/examples/company-app/starter/infra/k6/load.js) is a [k6](https://grafana.com/docs/k6/latest/) script that acts as five users for ten minutes: it lists employees and projects, asks for an employee that doesn't exist, and every tenth iteration creates and deletes an employee.

## What M5 Is

Milestone **M5** is an `assignments-service` that's as observable as employees-service:

| Signal | At M5 |
| --- | --- |

## Exercise

### Example: explore what the starters send

1. Start the app, if it isn't running, and start the load:

    ```bash
    docker compose up --build -d
    docker compose --profile load up -d load
    ```

    k6 runs for ten minutes and stops on its own. Run the second command again whenever a later exercise needs fresh traffic.

2. In the app at <http://localhost:8080>, create an employee, then create a project. Open Grafana at <http://localhost:3001> (log in with `admin` / `admin` if asked) and go to **Explore**. Pick each data source from the picker at the top of Explore, and answer the questions as you go.

3. **Traces.** Choose **Tempo**, keep the query type on **TraceQL**, and find requests that started in your browser:

    ```traceql
    {resource.service.name="web"}
    ```

    Open the trace for your `POST` to `/api/employees`. The waterfall draws one bar per span; click a span to see its **Span attributes** and **Resource attributes**.
    - Which services appear in the trace, and in what order? Which service did you expect to see that isn't there? (Look at `infra/proxy/nginx.conf`: is the proxy instrumented?)
    - The `web` span is the browser's `fetch`. How much longer is it than the employees-service server span under it, and where could the difference have gone?
    - Find the span from `notifications-service`. It ran in a different process, after a message crossed RabbitMQ. How did it end up in the same trace?

4. **Errors in traces.** k6 asks for an employee that doesn't exist every second. Find those requests:

    ```traceql
    {resource.service.name="employees-service" && span.http.response.status_code=404}
    ```

    Open one. Is the server span marked as an error? The [HTTP semantic conventions](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/specs/semconv/http/http-spans/#status) leave a server span's status unset for a 4xx response: the client asked for something that doesn't exist, and the server handled it correctly. Only a 5xx marks a server span as an error.

5. **Metrics.** Choose **Prometheus**, switch the query editor from **Builder** to **Code**, and run:

    ```promql
    http_server_request_duration_seconds_count{job="employees-service"}
    ```

    Each row in the results is a **series**: one combination of label values, with its own number.
    - How many series are there, and which labels tell them apart?
    - Compare the series for `GET api/employees` with the number of times k6 has listed employees. What is the value counting? Run the query again a minute later: what happened to it?
    - Now run the same query with `job="projects-service"`. It returns nothing. Open the **Metrics browser** (next to the query box in Code mode), search for `http_server`, and find projects-service's request metric. Why does a Python service have a different name for the same measurement? You'll come back to this in [Metrics](3-application-development/3.5.2-metrics.md).

6. **Logs.** Choose **Loki** and run `{service_name="employees-service"}`. Expand a line that says `Published employee.created`.
    - Which attributes come from the log message's template, and which did OpenTelemetry add?
    - Is there a `trace_id`? Copy it and paste it into a Tempo query. What do you get?

7. **The service graph.** In Tempo, switch the query type to **Service Graph**. Every edge is a pair of services that called each other in the last hour, built from the traces alone. Which edges match what you know about the Company app from 3.2, and what does the node called `user` stand for?

### Practice: find the gaps

The starters are instrumented well but not perfectly. Find two gaps with telemetry, not by reading code:

1. In Prometheus, run `{__name__=~"company_.*"}`. These are the business metrics, counted by the services' own code. Which services have one, and which service that creates things doesn't?
2. In Tempo, open a trace for `POST api/employees` and one for `DELETE api/employees/{id:guid}`:

    ```traceql
    {resource.service.name="employees-service" && span.http.request.method="DELETE"}
    ```

    Compare the spans under each server span. Which span does the create have that the delete doesn't?

Write both gaps down. You'll close them in [Metrics](3-application-development/3.5.2-metrics.md) and [Tracing](3-application-development/3.5.3-tracing.md).

### Apply: wire OpenTelemetry into assignments-service

1. **Before.** In the app, create an assignment. In Tempo, run `{resource.service.name="web" && span.http.request.method="POST"}` and open that trace.
    - The employees-service and projects-service spans are there, but each sits under a parent span that Grafana can't find. Which service's spans are missing?
    - Now create one with `curl` against the proxy, as in 3.3.5, and search for it. Nothing from employees-service or projects-service appears for that request at all. Even without OpenTelemetry, .NET forwards a `traceparent` header on outgoing calls, but with no SDK recording anything, the header says "not sampled", and the services downstream respect that and drop their spans too. One uninstrumented service in the middle breaks the whole trace.

2. **Packages.** From `assignments-service/src/AssignmentsService/`, add the same OpenTelemetry packages employees-service uses. Copy the exact versions from employees-service's `.csproj`, so the two services stay in step:

    ```bash
    dotnet add package OpenTelemetry.Extensions.Hosting --version 1.21.0
    dotnet add package OpenTelemetry.Exporter.OpenTelemetryProtocol --version 1.21.0
    dotnet add package OpenTelemetry.Instrumentation.AspNetCore --version 1.21.0
    dotnet add package OpenTelemetry.Instrumentation.Http --version 1.21.0
    dotnet add package OpenTelemetry.Instrumentation.Runtime --version 1.21.0
    dotnet add package Npgsql.OpenTelemetry --version 10.0.3
    ```

3. **Setup.** Copy `ServiceTelemetry.cs` and `TelemetryExtensions.cs` from employees-service's `Infrastructure/Telemetry/` into the same folder in assignments-service. Change the namespaces, `ServiceTelemetry.Name` to `"AssignmentsService"`, and the fallback service name to `"assignments-service"`. Read every line of `AddTelemetry` and be ready to explain:
    - why the health endpoints are filtered out of tracing;
    - what `AddSource("RabbitMQ.Client.*")` gives you, given that assignments both publishes and consumes;
    - why the exporter is only added when `OTEL_EXPORTER_OTLP_ENDPOINT` is set, and what that means for your tests.

4. **Register it.** In `Program.cs`, call `builder.AddTelemetry();` in the same place employees-service does, under an `// Observability` comment. The compose block from M2 already merges in `*otel` and sets `OTEL_SERVICE_NAME`, so no compose changes are needed.

5. **After.** Rebuild with `docker compose up --build -d assignments-service`, create another assignment in the UI, and open its trace. Draw the trace on paper as a tree: browser, assignments-service, the two HTTP calls, the two services they reach, and the database calls. Which of these spans did you write code for? Then check the service graph again: which new edges appeared?

6. **Run the tests.** `dotnet test` from `assignments-service/` should still pass with no collector running. If it doesn't, check step 3's last question.

> [!TIP]
> Compare your setup with M5's [`TelemetryExtensions.cs`](https://github.com/liatrio/engineering-bootcamp/tree/master/examples/company-app/milestones/M5-observable/assignments-service/src/AssignmentsService/Infrastructure/Telemetry/TelemetryExtensions.cs) and [`Program.cs`](https://github.com/liatrio/engineering-bootcamp/tree/master/examples/company-app/milestones/M5-observable/assignments-service/src/AssignmentsService/Program.cs) once yours works.

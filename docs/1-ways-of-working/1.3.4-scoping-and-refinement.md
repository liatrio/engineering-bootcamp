# Scoping & Refinement

Most work arrives as a vague request: _"We need a dashboard"_ or _"Customers keep complaining about login."_ Before a team can size or commit to it, the request has to become something small, clear, and valuable. That happens in two steps, repeated continuously as the team learns:

- **Scoping** agrees on the problem, who it's for, and where the edges are. It turns an idea into an epic with a clear outcome.
- **Refinement** breaks the epic into stories the team understands well enough to [size](1-ways-of-working/1.3.5-sizing.md) and start.

## Scoping

Agree on the problem before jumping to a solution. A well-scoped epic answers:

- **What problem are we solving, and for whom?**
- **What outcome tells us we succeeded?**
- **What's in scope, and what's explicitly out of scope?** Writing down what you won't do lets stakeholders disagree before work starts.
- **What's the smallest thing we could ship to learn?**
- **What are the constraints, assumptions, and risks?** Unknowns may need a [spike](1-ways-of-working/1.3.5-sizing.md#what-is-a-spike).

### Outcomes over outputs

An _output_ is something the team builds, such as a reporting page. An _outcome_ is the change it's meant to cause:

> An outcome is a change in human behavior that drives business results.
>
> _- Josh Seiden ([Intercom interview](https://www.intercom.com/blog/podcasts/josh-seiden-on-why-product-teams-should-focus-on-outcome-vs-output))_

Ask _"What will people be doing differently as a result?"_ Scoping around an outcome leaves room to find a simpler solution than the first one imagined.

### Start small and learn

Look for the smallest release that tells you whether you're on the right track. In Lean Startup this is the **minimum viable product (MVP)**: the version that gives the most validated learning for the least effort. The Agile Alliance notes that an MVP is about _learning_, not earning, and must still be viable enough to learn from ([Agile Alliance: MVP](https://www.agilealliance.org/glossary/mvp/)).

A [story map](https://www.agilealliance.org/glossary/story-mapping/) helps find that first release. Lay out the user's activities left to right, stack stories under each by priority, and the top row becomes a thin end-to-end version of the product, a _walking skeleton_. Like [vertical slicing](1-ways-of-working/1.3.3-stories.md#vertical-slicing), a thin slice that works end to end teaches you more than a finished layer that doesn't.

## Refinement

The [Scrum Guide](https://scrumguides.org/scrum-guide.html#product-backlog) defines refinement as _"the act of breaking down and further defining Product Backlog items into smaller more precise items."_ It isn't a formal Scrum event. Some teams hold a regular session, and others refine continuously as items near the top of the backlog. Either way, it's a team activity: the product owner brings priorities and context, and developers and testers spot risks and gaps. A [Three Amigos](https://www.agilealliance.org/glossary/three-amigos/) conversation, covering the business, development, and testing views, is a lightweight way to refine one story.

For each story near the top of the backlog, the team:

1. **Clarifies the intent.** Who is it for, and why does it matter?
2. **Writes [acceptance criteria](1-ways-of-working/1.3.3-stories.md#acceptance-criteria)**, including edge cases and error paths.
3. **[Splits](1-ways-of-working/1.3.3-stories.md#story-splitting) it** if it's too big.
4. **Records open questions and dependencies**, and who will answer them.
5. **[Sizes](1-ways-of-working/1.3.5-sizing.md) it** once it's clear enough.

Refine until the team knows the item will fit in a sprint, and only a sprint or two ahead. Mike Cohn warns that _"refining months ahead is often wasted effort"_ ([Mountain Goat Software](https://www.mountaingoatsoftware.com/agile/product-backlog/refinement)).

### Definition of Ready

Some teams agree a **Definition of Ready (DoR)**, criteria a story must meet before work starts, usually based on [INVEST](1-ways-of-working/1.3.3-stories.md#invest) ([Agile Alliance](https://www.agilealliance.org/glossary/definition-of-ready/)). It isn't part of Scrum, and it can backfire: Mike Cohn warns that a strict DoR becomes a stage gate, _"another way of describing a waterfall process,"_ and suggests guidelines rather than rules ([Mountain Goat Software](https://www.mountaingoatsoftware.com/agile/the-dangers-of-a-definition-of-ready)).

### Definition of Done

A **Definition of Done (DoD)** is a core part of Scrum. The [Scrum Guide](https://scrumguides.org/scrum-guide.html#commitment-definition-of-done) defines it as _"a formal description of the state of the Increment when it meets the quality measures required for the product."_ Work that doesn't meet it can't be released or presented at the Sprint Review. It goes back to the backlog. The DoD gives everyone the same meaning of "done", so "done" can't mean "done except for testing".

A story is complete only when it meets **both** the DoD and its own acceptance criteria:

| | Definition of Done | Acceptance criteria |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Applies to** | Every story the team delivers | One specific story |
| **Answers** | "Is this built to our quality standard?" | "Does this story do what was asked?" |
| **Changes** | Rarely; it tightens as the team matures | Written fresh for each story |

Keep the DoD to what applies to all work. For a team following the practices in this bootcamp, it might include:

- Acceptance criteria are met and verified
- Code is peer reviewed (see [Code Review](3-application-development/3.4.12-code-review.md))
- Automated tests are written and passing (see [Testing Concepts](3-application-development/3.4.1-testing-concepts.md))
- Linting and static analysis pass (see [Code Styling](3-application-development/3.4.9-code-styling.md))
- Changes are merged to the main branch and the CI pipeline is green
- Relevant documentation is updated

## Common pitfalls

- **Solutioning too early.** Debating implementation before agreeing on the problem and outcome.
- **Refining too far ahead.** Detailing work that may change or never be built.
- **Refining without the team.** A product owner writing stories alone misses technical risks.
- **Treating "ready" as a gate.** Handing work over a wall instead of collaborating on it.

## Exercise

Pick a vague request, such as _"Our team needs a way to share on-call schedules,"_ or one from your own work. Then:

1. Write a short scope: the problem, who it's for, the desired outcome (as a change in behavior), what's in and out of scope, and key risks.
2. Sketch a story map and identify the walking skeleton.
3. Refine that first slice into three to six stories using the [story format](1-ways-of-working/1.3.3-stories.md#story-format), each with acceptance criteria.
4. Check each story against INVEST. Split or rewrite any that fall short.
5. Record the epic and its stories in a work tracking tool (see [Work Tracking](1-ways-of-working/1.3.6-work-tracking.md)).

## Deliverable

- Present your scope and stories to a peer or mentor. Explain what you left out of scope and why.
- Discuss what your team's Definition of Done should include, and whether a Definition of Ready would help or hurt.

# Scoping & Refinement

Most work doesn't arrive as a well-written story. It arrives as a vague request: *"We need a dashboard,"* *"Customers keep complaining about login,"* *"Can we move this to the cloud?"* Before a team can size or commit to that work, it has to be turned into something small, clear, and valuable.

That happens in two steps:

- **Scoping** agrees on *what problem we are solving, for whom, and where the edges are*. It turns an idea into an epic with a clear outcome.
- **Refinement** breaks that epic into stories the team understands well enough to [size](1-ways-of-working/1.1.6-sizing.md) and pull into a sprint.

```text
Idea / request → Scope (epic) → Refine (ready stories) → Size → Plan → Build
```

Neither step happens once. Teams scope and refine continuously, a little ahead of the work, as they learn more.

## Scoping

Scoping is about agreeing on the problem before jumping to a solution. A well-scoped piece of work answers these questions:

| Question | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| **What problem are we solving, and for whom?** | Keeps the team focused on users rather than features |
| **What outcome tells us we succeeded?** | Gives you a way to measure value (see [Outcomes over outputs](#outcomes-over-outputs)) |
| **What is in scope, and what is explicitly out of scope?** | Surfaces disagreements early and limits scope creep |
| **What is the smallest thing we could ship to learn?** | Gets working software in front of users quickly (see [Start small and learn](#start-small-and-learn)) |
| **What are the constraints, assumptions, and risks?** | Exposes deadlines, compliance needs, and unknowns that may need a [spike](1-ways-of-working/1.1.6-sizing.md#what-is-a-spike) |

### Outcomes over outputs

An *output* is something a team builds, such as a new reporting page. An *outcome* is the result that output is meant to cause. Josh Seiden, author of *Outcomes Over Output*, defines it this way:

> An outcome is a change in human behavior that drives business results.
>
> *- Josh Seiden ([Intercom interview](https://www.intercom.com/blog/podcasts/josh-seiden-on-why-product-teams-should-focus-on-outcome-vs-output))*

A useful scoping question from the same interview is *"What will people be doing differently as a result of this feature?"* Scoping around an outcome, rather than a specific output, leaves room for the team to find a simpler solution than the one first imagined.

### Write down what's out of scope

Many teams add an explicit "out of scope" (or "not doing") list to an epic. Writing down what you've decided *not* to do records the decision, gives stakeholders a chance to disagree *before* work starts, and gives the team something to point to when new requests appear mid-sprint.

### Start small and learn

Look for the smallest release that lets you learn whether you're on the right track. In Lean Startup this is the **minimum viable product (MVP)**, which Eric Ries defined as the version of a new product that lets a team collect the maximum amount of validated learning about customers with the least effort. The Agile Alliance notes that an MVP focuses on *learning*, not earning, and warns against stressing "minimum" so much that the result isn't viable enough to learn from.

*- [Agile Alliance: Minimum Viable Product](https://www.agilealliance.org/glossary/mvp/)*

[Vertical slicing](1-ways-of-working/1.1.4-stories.md#vertical-slicing) applies here too: a thin slice that works end to end teaches you more than a finished layer that doesn't.

### Story mapping

Story mapping, developed by Jeff Patton, is a visual way for a team to scope an epic together. The map arranges user activities along the horizontal axis, roughly in the order the user performs them. Beneath each activity, user stories are stacked vertically by priority. The top row forms a minimal end-to-end version of the product (a *walking skeleton*), and each following row fleshes it out. This helps teams avoid releasing features that are unusable because they depend on lower-priority work that hasn't been built yet.

*- [Agile Alliance: Story Mapping](https://www.agilealliance.org/glossary/story-mapping/)*

## Refinement

The [Scrum Guide](https://scrumguides.org/scrum-guide.html#product-backlog) defines refinement as follows:

> Product Backlog refinement is the act of breaking down and further defining Product Backlog items into smaller more precise items. This is an ongoing activity to add details, such as a description, order, and size.

Refinement was formerly called *backlog grooming*. The Agile Alliance notes that the term has largely been replaced because of the word's negative connotations ([Agile Alliance: Backlog Refinement](https://www.agilealliance.org/glossary/backlog-refinement/)).

The Scrum Guide does not make refinement one of its formal events. Many teams hold a recurring refinement session (see [Scrum ceremonies](1-ways-of-working/1.1.1-scrum.md#ceremonies)), while others refine continuously as items approach the top of the backlog. Mike Cohn leans toward continuous refinement ([Mountain Goat Software: Backlog Refinement](https://www.mountaingoatsoftware.com/agile/product-backlog/refinement)). Either way, refinement is a team activity. The product owner brings priorities and context, and developers and testers spot technical risks and gaps. A [Three Amigos](https://www.agilealliance.org/glossary/three-amigos/) conversation, covering the business, development, and testing perspectives, is a lightweight way to refine a single story.

### What happens during refinement

The Agile Alliance lists the typical refinement activities as removing stories that are no longer relevant, creating new stories for newly discovered needs, re-assessing priority, estimating stories that have no estimate yet, correcting estimates as new information appears, and splitting high-priority stories that are too large for an upcoming iteration ([Agile Alliance: Backlog Refinement](https://www.agilealliance.org/glossary/backlog-refinement/)).

In practice, for each story near the top of the backlog, the team:

1. **Clarifies the intent.** Who is it for, and why does it matter?
2. **Writes or sharpens [acceptance criteria](1-ways-of-working/1.1.4-stories.md#acceptance-criteria)**, including edge cases and error paths.
3. **[Splits](1-ways-of-working/1.1.4-stories.md#story-splitting) stories that are too big.**
4. **Records open questions and dependencies**, including who needs to answer each question.
5. **Spikes unknowns.** If the team can't agree on an approach, it creates a timeboxed [spike](1-ways-of-working/1.1.6-sizing.md#what-is-a-spike) rather than guessing.
6. **[Sizes](1-ways-of-working/1.1.6-sizing.md) the story** once it's clear enough to estimate. The Scrum Guide says the developers who will do the work are responsible for sizing.

Mike Cohn's rule of thumb for when to stop: *refine until the team knows the item will fit in a sprint* ([Mountain Goat Software](https://www.mountaingoatsoftware.com/agile/product-backlog/refinement)). Stories that aren't ready stay in the backlog with their open questions recorded.

### Definition of Ready

The Scrum Guide's test for readiness is simple: items that can be Done by the team within one sprint are *"deemed ready for selection"* in Sprint Planning ([Scrum Guide](https://scrumguides.org/scrum-guide.html#product-backlog)).

Some teams go further and agree on a **Definition of Ready (DoR)**: explicit criteria, generally based on [INVEST](1-ways-of-working/1.1.4-stories.md#invest), that a story must meet before the team accepts it into an iteration. A DoR gives the team an agreed basis for pushing back on ill-defined work ([Agile Alliance: Definition of Ready](https://www.agilealliance.org/glossary/definition-of-ready/)). An example DoR might include:

- The story meets the INVEST criteria
- Acceptance criteria are written and understood by the team
- Known dependencies on other teams or vendors are identified
- The team has sized it, and it fits within a sprint

!> A Definition of Ready is not part of the Scrum Guide, and it can backfire. Mike Cohn warns that requiring work to be 100% complete before a story enters a sprint turns the DoR into a stage gate, *"another way of describing a waterfall process."* He recommends that most teams skip a formal DoR, and that teams who use one favor guidelines over rules ([Mountain Goat Software: The Dangers of a Definition of Ready](https://www.mountaingoatsoftware.com/agile/the-dangers-of-a-definition-of-ready)).

### Definition of Done

Where a Definition of Ready is optional, a **Definition of Done (DoD)** is a core part of Scrum. The [Scrum Guide](https://scrumguides.org/scrum-guide.html#commitment-definition-of-done) defines it as follows:

> The Definition of Done is a formal description of the state of the Increment when it meets the quality measures required for the product.

Key points from the Scrum Guide:

- **Done means releasable quality.** Work cannot be considered part of an Increment unless it meets the Definition of Done. An item that doesn't meet it *"cannot be released or even presented at the Sprint Review"* and returns to the Product Backlog.
- **It creates transparency.** Everyone shares an understanding of what "done" means, so "done" can't mean "done except for testing."
- **The team owns it.** If the organization has a standard DoD, all teams follow it as a minimum. Otherwise, the Scrum Team creates one appropriate for the product. Multiple teams working on the same product share one DoD.

The Agile Alliance adds that the DoD should be an explicit, visible list the whole team agrees on, not just a shared understanding, and that work not meeting it at the end of a sprint should not count toward that sprint's velocity ([Agile Alliance: Definition of Done](https://www.agilealliance.org/glossary/definition-of-done/)).

#### Definition of Done vs. acceptance criteria

The two work together but answer different questions:

| | Definition of Done | Acceptance criteria |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Applies to** | Every story the team delivers | One specific story |
| **Answers** | "Is this work built to our quality standard?" | "Does this story do what was asked?" |
| **Owned by** | The whole team (or the organization) | Agreed by the product owner and team during refinement |
| **Changes** | Rarely; it tightens as the team matures | Written fresh for each story |

A story is complete only when it meets **both** its [acceptance criteria](1-ways-of-working/1.1.4-stories.md#acceptance-criteria) and the team's DoD. As the Agile Alliance notes, individual stories may have specific "done" criteria in addition to the general list.

#### Example Definition of Done

Each team writes its own DoD. Keep it to the minimum work that applies to everything; the Agile Alliance warns that obsessing over the list is counter-productive. A DoD for a team following the practices in this chapter might include:

- Acceptance criteria are met and verified
- Code is peer reviewed (see [Code Review](1-ways-of-working/1.4.8-code-review.md))
- Unit and functional tests are written and passing (see [Unit Testing](1-ways-of-working/1.4.1-unit-testing.md) and [Functional Testing](1-ways-of-working/1.4.2-functional-testing.md))
- Linting, styling, and static analysis checks pass (see [Code Styling](1-ways-of-working/1.4.3-code-styling.md) and [SonarQube](1-ways-of-working/1.4.7-sonarqube.md))
- Changes are merged to the main branch and the CI pipeline is green
- Relevant documentation is updated
- The product owner has reviewed the change

### A healthy backlog

Roman Pichler describes a well-maintained product backlog as **DEEP** ([Roman Pichler: Make the Product Backlog DEEP](https://www.romanpichler.com/blog/make-the-product-backlog-deep/)):

| Characteristic | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **D**etailed appropriately | Items at the top are detailed and ready; lower-priority items can stay coarse |
| **E**stimated | Items are sized so the team can plan and forecast |
| **E**mergent | The backlog evolves as the team learns: items are added, changed, and removed |
| **P**rioritized | The most valuable items are at the top |

How far ahead should you refine? Mike Cohn advises that *"the next sprint or two should be clear enough to execute. Items further down should be vague enough to change,"* and that *"refining months ahead is often wasted effort"* ([Mountain Goat Software](https://www.mountaingoatsoftware.com/agile/product-backlog/refinement)).

## Common pitfalls

- **Solutioning too early.** Debating implementation before agreeing on the problem and outcome.
- **Refining too far ahead.** Detailing work that may never be prioritized, or that will change before it's built.
- **Refining without the team.** A product owner writing stories alone misses technical risks and testing concerns.
- **Treating "ready" as a gate.** Using a DoR to hand work over a wall instead of collaborating on it.
- **Never revisiting scope.** New information should change the plan. Responding to change is the point of being agile.

## Exercise

Pick a vague request, such as *"Our team needs a way to share on-call schedules,"* or one from your own work. Then:

1. Write a short scope for it: the problem, who it's for, the desired outcome (as a change in behavior), what's in scope, what's out of scope, and key risks or assumptions.
2. Sketch a story map of the user's journey and identify the walking skeleton: the top row that works end to end.
3. Refine that first slice into three to six stories using the [story format](1-ways-of-working/1.1.4-stories.md#story-format), each with acceptance criteria.
4. Check each story against INVEST. Split or rewrite any that fall short.
5. Record the epic and its stories in a work tracking tool (see [Work Tracking](1-ways-of-working/1.2-work-tracking.md)).

## Deliverable

- Present your scope and stories to a peer or mentor. Explain what you left out of scope and why.
- Discuss how scoping and refinement reduce surprises during a sprint, whether your team would benefit from a Definition of Ready, and what your team's Definition of Done should include.

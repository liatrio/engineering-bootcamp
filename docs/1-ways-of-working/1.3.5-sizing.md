# Sizing

![agile image](img/agile-points.svg ':size=380 :class=img-center :alt= agile image')

Neither framework prescribes how to estimate. The Scrum Guide says the Developers who will do the work are responsible for sizing it, and leaves the method to them. Kanban doesn't require estimates at all, and forecasts from cycle time instead (see [Kanban](1-ways-of-working/1.3.2-kanban.md#flow-metrics)).

Many teams, especially Scrum teams, still size stories to decide how much to bring into a sprint. **It is easier to judge the relative size of a task than to figure out how much time it will take.**

## Story pointing

Story points come from Extreme Programming (XP). They're relative to a team: rather than tracking hours, a point value compares the effort of one story with another. Elements that affect the estimate are:

- uncertainty
- complexity
- security risks
- size
- dependencies

> [!NOTE]
> As a team gets better at [story splitting](1-ways-of-working/1.3.3-stories.md#story-splitting), its stories get smaller, aiming for about a day of work each, and sizing gets simpler.

## Fibonacci technique

![A Fibonacci spiral split into segments with each segment labeled by a number in the Fibonacci sequence. 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13](img/agile-fib.svg)

People estimate stories with smaller points more accurately than stories with higher points. As the numbers increase, the difference between two succeeding numbers increases exponentially and leads to less accurate estimates. This is intentional, as most stories are often more complex than we realize.

_- [Wikipedia: Fibonacci Scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_scale_(agile))_

As teams adopt relative sizing, they must come to a common understanding about the size of a 1 point story, the base unit of work. [Planning Poker](https://www.agilealliance.org/glossary/planning-poker/) is a common way to do this. Each team member picks an estimate in secret, everyone reveals at once, and the people with the highest and lowest estimates explain their reasoning before the team estimates again. It gives everyone a voice and builds the team's shared baseline.

## What is a spike?

In Extreme Programming (XP), a [spike solution](http://www.extremeprogramming.org/rules/spike.html) is a very simple program to explore potential solutions to a tough technical or design problem. It addresses only the problem under examination, and most spikes are thrown away. The goal is to reduce technical risk, or to make a story's estimate more reliable. Spikes should answer a specific question and be timeboxed.

Spikes represent a trade-off between a need to answer questions and a need to produce features. Product owners and delivery teams should carefully consider how much time and energy to devote to spikes, ensuring that the time is spent creating value by solving a technical problem or reducing uncertainty.
